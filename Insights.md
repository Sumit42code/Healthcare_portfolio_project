# Healthcare Access and Service Performance Insights

## Executive Summary

- Demand is stable, but access and flow are constrained. Referral volume increased only 0.44% and ED volume 0.34% in the comparable January–July periods, yet 89.47% of the referral backlog had been waiting more than 60 days.
- Appointment execution is the clearest operational priority. Only 29.89% of eligible appointments met the department waiting-time target, while the network no-show rate was 12.83%. Dermatology was the weakest specialty for target performance and also had the highest specialty-level no-show rate.
- ED activity is evenly distributed, but patient-flow indicators require review. Admissions represented 20.83% of visits, 5.10% left before treatment, and 39.85% of visits with a previous recorded discharge returned within seven days.
- Management should focus on throughput rather than demand growth alone. The strongest near-term opportunities are reducing old referral backlog, improving urgent referral access, redesigning Dermatology and ENT clinic flow, reducing avoidable no-shows, and investigating repeat ED use and left-before-treatment patterns.

## Referral access: stable demand is not translating into timely access

Referral intake remained stable, but the backlog is old and target performance is weak. The operational question is therefore less about accommodating rapid demand growth and more about whether referral processing, specialist capacity, booking and closure activity are keeping pace.

### 1. Referral volume remained stable

The network recorded 32,000 referrals, averaging 1,684 per month. January–July volume increased from 11,698 in 2025 to 11,749 in 2026, a rise of only 0.44%. February was the lowest month in both years, but its daily rate was similar to longer months; the lower total is mainly consistent with fewer calendar days rather than a material fall in demand.

Completed referrals represented 49.94% of all records, while Waiting and Open referrals together represented 25.11%. Management can use roughly 1,680 referrals per month as a planning baseline, but should track bookings and closures alongside new referrals. Stable intake combined with a rising or ageing backlog would point to a throughput constraint rather than an intake surge.

![Monthly referrals by status](assets/chart-000.png)

### 2. The referral backlog requires both network-wide and targeted action

The network had 8,035 backlog referrals. Of the 5,888 referrals with enough information for target assessment, 4,924 were over target, producing an 83.63% over-target rate. Lakes District Hospital Respiratory had the largest departmental backlog at 238 referrals, with a 276.55-day average wait and 84.75% of eligible referrals over target.

The top 10 departments accounted for only 27.42% of the backlog, and regional shares were close to 25% each. This is not an isolated department problem. Management should run a network backlog-reduction programme while prioritising departments that combine high volume, long waits, high over-target rates and urgent clinical demand.

### 3. Backlog age is more concerning than backlog size alone

As at August 1, 2026, 7,189 referrals—89.47% of the backlog—had waited more than 60 days. Only 10.53% had waited 60 days or less. The over-60-day share ranged narrowly from 88.78% in Midland to 90.27% in Southern, confirming a network-wide ageing problem.

The immediate priority should be a clinically risk-adjusted review of the oldest referrals, not a simple first-in-first-out clearance exercise. The network should also monitor whether new referrals entering the backlog exceed those being booked, completed, cancelled or closed.

![Referral backlog by waiting-time band](assets/chart-001.png)

### 4. Only half of referrals received a first appointment within target

Of 14,674 target-eligible referrals, 7,420 met the target, an overall achievement rate of 50.57%. Routine referrals performed slightly better than urgent referrals (51.15% versus 47.21%). Although urgent referrals had shorter average waits, their tighter service targets meant that more than half still missed the required timeframe.

Midland's urgent pathway was the weakest regional result, with a 44.29% target-met rate and a 91.43-day average wait. Management should review urgent-referral triage, specialist capacity, booking lead time, cancellations and unfilled appointment slots, starting with Midland and then applying the same review across the network.

## Appointment performance: capacity is being lost before and during clinics

Appointment volumes increased, but no-shows and delays reduce the effective capacity available to patients. The evidence supports a combined strategy: network-wide attendance improvements plus specialty-specific clinic-flow redesign.

### 5. Appointment activity increased, but the July spike needs validation

The historical period contained 61,188 appointments, of which 74.70% were completed. January–July volume increased 21.40% year over year, but excluding the unusually high July 2026 result reduces the increase to 12.96%. July 2026 recorded 5,389 appointments, more than 60% above both the previous month and July 2025.

The increase may represent added clinics or backlog-reduction activity, but it could also reflect a recording change. Management should confirm the cause before treating it as sustainable capacity. The data also contains 232 scheduled appointments from October 2026 to January 2027, outside the project's documented future window; the coverage rule and these records should be reconciled before final reporting.

### 6. No-shows are a network issue with identifiable department hotspots

There were 6,729 no-shows among 52,438 attendance-eligible appointments, a network rate of 12.83%. Otago Valley Hospital Cardiology had the highest department rate at 15.78%, while Waitemata Community Hospital General Surgery had the largest no-show count among the highest-rate departments, with 197.

Regional rates differed by only 0.42 percentage points, and the top 10 departments contributed 27.17% of no-shows. A network-wide reminder, confirmation and easy-rescheduling programme is therefore justified, with targeted support for the highest-rate departments. A one-percentage-point reduction would represent approximately 524 additional attended appointments, assuming eligible volume remained unchanged.

![Highest department no-show rates](assets/chart-002.png)

### 7. Waiting-time target performance is weak across every specialty

Only 13,443 of 44,969 eligible appointments met target, an achievement rate of 29.89%. The weighted average wait was 44.83 minutes, but no department achieved a rate above 50%. Dermatology was the clear outlier at 15.40%, followed by ENT at 24.67%; other specialties were clustered near 32%–34%. Because average waits were similar across specialties, the gap may reflect different target thresholds as well as clinic processes. Management should validate target definitions and examine clinic start times, staffing, room availability and scheduling. Median and 90th-percentile waits should be added because an average can hide a long-waiting minority.

![Appointment waiting-time target achievement by specialty](assets/chart-003.png)

### 8. Booking channel explains only a small part of no-show variation

Phone bookings had the highest no-show rate at 13.41%, compared with 12.49% for Clinic bookings, a difference of 0.92 percentage points. If Phone bookings had matched the Clinic rate, approximately 121 no-shows would have been avoided over the period.

The Referral Team produced the largest no-show count (2,982) because it handled the largest volume, not because it had the highest rate. The operational response should therefore combine targeted confirmation for Phone bookings with high-volume interventions for Referral Team bookings. Since all channels were above 12%, booking channel alone does not explain the wider attendance problem.

### 9. Deprivation differences are small and not consistently linear

Among 52,202 appointments with known deprivation information, the no-show rate was 12.82%. Quintile 5 had the highest rate at 13.22%, but Quintile 2 was also relatively high at 13.13%, while Quintiles 3 and 4 were lower. The results do not show a steady increase across deprivation levels.

Quintile 5 was 0.49 percentage points above Quintile 1, equivalent to approximately 52 additional no-shows at the observed volume. This is worth monitoring as an equity signal, but it is not enough to claim that deprivation causes non-attendance. The 236 eligible appointments with missing deprivation information should also be reported separately rather than silently excluded.

## Emergency care: stable volume does not remove flow and continuity risks

ED volume was stable and evenly distributed, but average length of stay, leaving before treatment and repeat visits show where operational review should move next. These indicators must be interpreted alongside clinical severity and disposition.

### 10. Monthly ED demand is stable across the network

The network recorded 22,000 ED visits, averaging 1,158 per month. January–July activity increased from 8,058 visits in 2025 to 8,085 in 2026, only 0.34%. Regional totals were also close, ranging from 5,439 in Southern to 5,529 in Midland.

The current evidence does not support a major capacity increase based on monthly volume growth alone. Staffing decisions should instead use arrivals by hour and weekday, triage severity, length of stay and admission demand. Daily rates should be used when comparing months of different lengths.

![Monthly emergency department visits](assets/chart-004.png)

### 11. ED length of stay is similar across facilities, but extreme cases need review

There were 21,268 length-of-stay-eligible visits, with an estimated network average of 5.65 hours. Facility averages ranged from 5.50 hours at Pounamu Regional Hospital to 5.74 hours at Kowhai Central Hospital, a spread of about 14 minutes.

The small gap suggests a network-wide pattern rather than a single underperforming site. Otago Valley Hospital recorded the longest individual stay at 29.57 hours, but a maximum is not representative of normal performance. Management should add median and 90th-percentile length of stay and segment the results by disposition, triage and presenting condition. No formal LOS target was supplied, so this analysis cannot label the current average as acceptable or unacceptable.

### 12. Admissions are consistent, while leaving before treatment varies more

Admissions accounted for 4,582 visits (20.83%), while 1,121 visits (5.10%) ended with the patient leaving before treatment. Facility admission rates were tightly grouped from 20.30% to 21.78%, but left-before-treatment rates ranged from 4.01% at Lakes District Hospital to 5.97% at Rimu Regional Hospital.

Rimu and Waitemata should be reviewed for arrival peaks, triage-to-clinician delay, staffing coverage and waiting-room congestion. Admission rate should not be treated as a quality ranking because it may reflect case mix and acuity. Thirty visits had an unmatched facility and should remain in network totals but not facility rankings.

### 13. Four presenting groups account for nearly three-quarters of ED demand

Injury was the largest group with 5,289 visits (24.04%). Injury, abdominal and respiratory presentations together represented 59.96% of ED visits; adding infection increased the combined share to 73.52%.

Capacity planning should ensure appropriate staffing, pathways and equipment for these high-volume presentations. Respiratory demand should be reviewed seasonally. Cardiac and neurological presentations have lower volume but may be more urgent, so volume should not be used as the only prioritisation measure. The Other category, at 8.05%, should be reviewed to determine whether useful subgroups are being hidden.

![Emergency department visits by presenting group](assets/chart-005.png)

### 14. Seven-day repeat ED use is high across every facility

Among 10,311 visits with a previous recorded discharge, 4,109 returned within seven days, a rate of 39.85%. Harbour Regional Hospital had the highest facility rate at 42.28%, followed by Pounamu Regional Hospital at 41.73%; Waitemata Community Hospital was lowest at 37.55%.

The 4.73-point facility range is smaller than the high network level, so the issue appears broad rather than isolated. Management should investigate repeat presentations by condition, triage, discharge disposition, age, deprivation and follow-up access. This measure is not an inpatient readmission rate, does not use all ED visits as its denominator, and groups each return under the current facility even when the previous visit occurred elsewhere.

![Seven-day repeat ED visit rate by current facility](assets/chart-006.png)

## Recommended Next Steps

1. Within 30 days — confirm the evidence base. Reconcile the July 2026 appointment spike, the 232 appointments beyond the documented future window, unmatched facility and region records, and the definitions for waiting-time and repeat-visit measures.
2. Within 30–60 days — protect clinically urgent access. Risk-stratify the over-60-day referral backlog, review Midland urgent referrals, and create department-level reduction targets that balance age, urgency and volume.
3. Within 60–90 days — recover appointment capacity. Pilot reminders and confirmation for Phone and Referral Team bookings, introduce easy cancellation and short-notice refill lists, and redesign Dermatology and ENT clinic flow.
4. Within 60–90 days — investigate ED flow and continuity. Review left-before-treatment patterns at Rimu and Waitemata, examine extreme length-of-stay cases, and identify the presenting groups and patient cohorts driving seven-day returns.
5. Ongoing — monitor a balanced scorecard. Track demand, backlog age, target achievement, no-shows, median and 90th-percentile waits, ED length of stay, left-before-treatment and repeat visits. Pair counts with rates and denominators so high-volume services are not misclassified as poor performers.
